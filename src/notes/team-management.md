Releasing the full potential of the management team is a central task of the responsible executive and one of his principal obligations regardless of his rank or assignment.

effectiveness in administration

the idea of "effectiveness" includes not only success in competition and profitable operation but also the responsible role of the business- man in community and world affairs, as a citizen and as a leader obligated to serve the larger community outside his own company. And the idea of "administration" grows with the imagination and aspiration which is applied to its study in the many concrete experiences that the Harvard Business School shares with business, all helping to clarify and make tangible and practical the worthiest connotations of business leadership and the professional character of administration.

Delegation and control are the principal functions for the development of the greatest potential effectiveness of a management team.

free communication must be achieved before extensive delegation and effective control become possible.

Knowing how to delegate, how to exercise control, and how to achieve free communication are essential skills of the executive.

We begin to believe that delegation better serves its purpose when we take chances on subordinates than when we hesitate for fear they will fall short. Responsibility can probably be learned only when it is fully given

that the strength of any organization or any part of an organization increases with the ability of people at all levels to accept responsibility.

The administrator delegates responsibility to the people below him. By the exercise of control over the activities delegated, he is informed of the quality of the work delegated. He then gives attention to qualifying men to discharge better the responsibility delegated to them. Rather than hold the responsibilities assigned to any person or any group at a static level, he tries to arrange that these responsibilities should continually exceed the capacities of individuals by a sufficient margin to challenge them to reach forward for strength and upward in the quality of their contribution to the company.

Realizing the potentialities of the organizations in which we work means not only success and strength to our companies but the attainment as well of our full powers as individuals. Essential to satisfying participation in organized affairs is our realization that we are continually moving forward in our grasp of what we are doing and what needs to be done.

delegation we are dividing the responsibilities which accrue in the management of a business into compartments headed by individuals who are given the required authority to deal with the responsibilities which are theirs and to represent these responsibilities in their associations within the management group

if delegation of authority is to be successful, then the responsibility and the degree of authority to be delegated must be spelled out in clear, unmistakable terms, so that not only the individual to whom responsibility is delegated will know the limits of his authority, but also that others in the organization will have a clear understanding.

There is always the easy path: saying to yourself that once a responsibility is assigned to a specific executive, it no longer concerns you except to replace the man who fails in execution.

Responsibility can be delegated with the requisite grant of authority to permit its exercise. However, the ultimate responsibility for placing responsibility in proper hands and the supervision required to determine the extent and degree to which delegation is effective can never be delegated. The chief executive whose company continues to grow will seldom be found in his office; he will be visiting not only within his office but also in field offices and plants. Through such visits he begins to know the individuals who form the team, to find out if communication from top to bottom is effective, to determine those within the executive chain who have capacities for growth.

No man can be judged fairly through another man's eyes; no man can be judged better than in the exercise of responsibility at his place of work.

Junior executives have become willing to talk freely and informally, eager to express their thoughts and ideas on how the company can improve. These talks must never lead to criticism of others; rather they must be led into discussions of company policies and procedures.

men entering into higher executive positions have the basic information and knowledge which they need but lack the confidence of experience and are unwilling to back their own judgments against those of associates who appear to be more mature and to have more experience.

The human touch at the right time is of equal importance.

Another important factor to sound understanding which can never be overdone is the ability to give the responsible executives full credit for the results which follow the exercise of good judgment.

Any executive who is deeply interested in his organization often resorts to the planting of ideas among his immediate assistants so that, as those ideas develop into reality, credit for both the idea and its execution may be given to these assistants.

Business can be fun; in fact it must be fun for those who are working together if the maximum effort is to result.

Each executive in the chain must know his job to be able to subdivide and delegate his responsibilities.

He must know the individuals to whom he is giving authority to be sure that it is within their capacity, or to be sure that he can develop their capacity to exercise it properly.

He must have a constant flow of statistical information to his desk to enable him to measure the performance of those to whom he has delegated authority.

He must visit them frequency to judge their performance on the job and how they in turn are delegating authority and developing subordinates who will be capable of accepting further responsibilities.

He must hold down the brash without destroying their confidence, draw out the timid without making their decisions, give credit to those who deserve it, take the blame for those who fail until repeated failure has demonstrated their unworthiness, be patient when judgments prove wrong, and be angry and impatient only when mishaps occur because decisions have not been made.

He must be accessible to all who work under him, sufficiently free in his relationships to have their confidence; and yet he must maintain an indefinable reserve — a dignity of position consistent with that wise old axiom "familiarity breeds contempt."

To do all this, he must be the master of the position he occupies and be fully informed about the progress of the activities under his direction. He must be the buffer between his organization and the public; and he must earn and hold a reputation and a public standing which entitle him to the respect of his associates.

If he is and can do these things, then indeed he can fit the chart to the individual, or the individual to the chart, with the flexibility that turns an organization on paper into an effective management team.

increasingly there is evidence of a growing understanding of the importance of human relationships to the success of any organization.

the executive must know his people, for only in that knowledge can he approach with hope of success the selection of those to exercise authority. If he has selected them properly, he will find it almost impossible to retain the authorities and responsibilities which he should have given up long ago.
